## 1. Standard weather station setup and siting (WMO / Met Office / school practice)

### Takeaway
Thermometers go in a white, louvred Stevenson screen with bulbs 1.25–2 m above ground, and the door opens toward the pole: north in the Northern Hemisphere, south in Zimbabwe. The UK manual rain gauge has a 5-inch (127 mm) rim set 30 cm above ground. Wind instruments go at 10 m over open ground, and wind is named for the direction it blows FROM. Sun and wind are the main siting enemies, so instruments are kept away from obstacles.

### Cited Findings
**Stevenson screen**
- Thermometer bulbs sit between 1.25 m and 2 m above ground level. The screen is "always mounted on a stand at such a height that the thermometer bulbs are between 1.25 m and 2 m above ground level" so that stations can be compared — [Environment Canada MANCLIM manual](https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/weather-manuals-documentation/manclim-climatological-observations/temperature.html)
- Screen height varies by country but "generally within the WMO prescribed measurement height of 1.25–2.0 m". The Netherlands lowered its screens from about 2.20 m to 1.50 m around 1960 after a WMO regulation — [KNMI Technical Report TR-400](https://cdn.knmi.nl/knmi/pdf/bibliotheek/knmipubTR/TR400.pdf) (search extract)
- Siting rule of thumb (Environment Canada): put the screen at least twice as far from any object as the object is tall, e.g. 20 m from a 10 m tree. WMO (2010) recommendations are cited as the basis for siting — [Wikipedia: Stevenson screen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stevenson_screen) (search extract)
- Door orientation: the door faces north in the Northern Hemisphere and is reversed (faces south) in the Southern Hemisphere, so direct sun does not fall on the thermometers when the door is opened — [Pakistan Meteorological Department](https://metkhi.pmd.gov.pk/stevenson-screen.php); [Weather for Schools – Setting up your weather station](https://weatherforschools.me.uk/html/settingup.html) (search extracts). Some Wikipedia-derived text gives only the Northern Hemisphere "face north" rule.
- White paint reflects solar radiation and is usually renewed about every two years. Louvres let air flow freely so the thermometers measure air temperature, not sunshine — [WeatherOnline: The Stevenson Screen](https://www.weatheronline.pl/reports/wxfacts/The-Stevenson-Screen.htm) (search extract)
- Hobbyist and school compromise: many amateur stations cannot meet the WMO range, so guidance suggests at least 1.5 m and as far from buildings as practical — [Aguilmard knowledge base: observation standards](https://www.aguilmard.com/kb/observation-standards/) (secondary, non-official)

**Rain gauge**
- Met Office standard gauge: a sharp brass or steel rim, 5 inches (127 mm) in diameter, set 30 cm above ground. A funnel drains into a narrow-necked bottle inside a removable can. The collected water is poured into a graduated measuring cylinder to read in mm — [Met Office: How we measure rainfall](https://weather.metoffice.gov.uk/guides/observations/how-we-measure-rainfall) (search extract; the bottle and can details are from the extract, and the measuring-cylinder step is standard practice that was not verified on the page)
- The rim is one foot (about 30 cm) above ground to prevent rain splashing in. Historically the gauge was copper, and the 1998 pattern is stainless steel — [Science Museum Group: Standard pattern Met Office rain gauge, 1998](https://collection.sciencemuseumgroup.org.uk/objects/co8656219/glass-bottle-for-standard-pattern-meteorological-office-rain-gauge-1998)
- FAO: "normal height of exposure is usually 30 cm above ground level. At greater height wind affects the accuracy of measurement" — [FAO T7202E](https://www.fao.org/3/T7202E/t7202e09.htm) (search extract)
- The Met Office has used turf walls around gauges at windy sites, but "it is not the present practice to build turf walls at new stations" — [Met Office: How we measure rainfall](https://weather.metoffice.gov.uk/guides/observations/how-we-measure-rainfall) (search extract)

**Wind (anemometer and vane)**
- WMO standard: anemometer at 10 m above open, flat terrain, with no obstructions within 10 times the obstruction's height — [Aguilmard: observation standards](https://www.aguilmard.com/kb/observation-standards/) (secondary)
- Most stations in NOAA's ISH dataset have the anemometer "at the standard height of 10 m" — [NIST technical note on extreme winds](https://itl.nist.gov/div898/winds/NIST_TN/nist_tn.htm) (search extract)
- Wind direction "always describes the direction from which the wind is blowing" — [Oklahoma Mesonet: wind measurements](https://mesonet.org/about/instruments/wind-measurements). WMO code tables define the parameter as "wind direction (from which blowing)" — [ECMWF parameter database: 10 m wind direction](https://confluence.ecmwf.int/spaces/UER/pages/51712433/10+metre+wind+direction)

**Six's (maximum–minimum) thermometer**
- U-shaped tube. The bulb on the minimum side holds alcohol. A mercury column pushes small steel/iron indices that stay at the highest and lowest points reached. A magnet resets the indices — [Wikipedia: Six's thermometer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Six%27s_thermometer); [AMS Glossary: Six's thermometer](https://glossary.ametsoc.org/wiki/six-s-thermometer/) (search extracts)
- Mercury freezes at −38.8 °C, which sets the instrument's lower limit. Mercury-free school versions exist and are safer — [Wikipedia: Six's thermometer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Six%27s_thermometer); [Weather for Schools: Six's max/min thermometers](https://www.weatherforschools.me.uk/html/maxmin.html) (search extracts)

**Campbell–Stokes sunshine recorder**
- A glass sphere focuses sunlight onto an hour-marked card, and the burn's intensity depends on brightness. As the sun moves across the sky the burn trace moves along the card. Three overlapping sets of grooves hold different cards for different seasons, to match the sun's height — [Whipple Museum, Cambridge](https://collections.whipplemuseum.cam.ac.uk/objects/10594); [An Iodhlann (Tiree) Met Office instrument](https://www.aniodhlann.org.uk/object/2001-153-1/) (search extracts)
- History: designed by J. F. Campbell in 1853 and modified by Sir George Stokes; sources give 1879 or 1880 for Met Office adoption. In 1884 a sphere in a yoke made it adjustable for any latitude. Reading the card involves the observer's judgement — [Met Office Library record](https://library.metoffice.gov.uk/Portal/Default/en-GB/recordview/index/631882); [Casella manual](https://www.AnalogWeather.com/uploads/7/7/7/5/77750690/casella_campbell_stokes_sunshine_recorder_manual.pdf) (search extracts; the 1879/1880 date conflict is in the sources)

**Wet- and dry-bulb hygrometer (psychrometer)**
- Two identical thermometers, one with its bulb wrapped in a wet muslin wick. Evaporation removes heat, so the wet bulb reads lower. Drier air means more evaporation and a larger "wet-bulb depression". In saturated air both read the same. Tables convert the two readings to relative humidity and dew point — [Britannica: psychrometer](https://britannica.com/technology/psychrometer); [U.S. Centennial of Flight: psychrometer](https://centennialofflight.net/essay/Dictionary/Psychrometer/DI69.htm) (search extracts)

### Inferences
- For a Zimbabwe (Southern Hemisphere) school animation, the Stevenson screen door should face south, so the sun, which is mostly to the north, does not shine in.
- A school Stevenson screen with bulbs at about 1.25–1.5 m sits within the WMO range. Note that the UK Met Office has used about 1.25 m, but that specific figure was not verified here.
- Show the rain gauge funnel rim at about 30 cm (knee height) over short grass, clear of buildings and trees.
- A school wind vane is often mounted lower than 10 m on a pole or roof. That is a practical departure from the WMO standard and worth flagging in the film.

### Gaps
- **Barometer indoors:** I found no source stating this. It is standard textbook practice, since pressure is the same indoors and out and the instrument is protected from weather, but it is unverified here.
- **Surface:** "Short grass" under the screen is widely stated, but no fetched source confirmed it.
- **Exact Met Office screen height:** about 1.25 m is the commonly quoted figure but was not verified.
- **Wording of WMO-No. 8:** the primary text, including its siting classes, was not retrieved.
- **Graduated measuring-cylinder detail:** the 127 mm cylinder graduations were not verified from a source.

## 2. Clouds (WMO International Cloud Atlas) and oktas

### Takeaway
The WMO recognises 10 cloud genera, and each cloud belongs to exactly one. Nimbostratus is the grey, often dark, Sun-blotting layer from which more or less continuous rain or snow reaches the ground. Cumulonimbus is the thunderstorm cloud. Cloud cover is reported in oktas (eighths of the sky).

### Cited Findings
- "The classification of clouds has ten main groups, called genera. Each observed cloud is a member of one, and only one, genus." Root words: cirrus = fibre/hair, cumulus = heap/pile, stratus = layer/sheet — [WMO: Classifying clouds](https://wmo.int/world-meteorological-day-2017/classifying-clouds); [WMO Cloud Atlas: cloud classification summary](https://cloudatlas.wmo.int/en/cloud-classification-summary.html) (search extracts)
- Atlas labels from the classification summary: cumulus is a heap-like, cumuliform cloud. Stratus is a uniform layer. Cumulonimbus is the thunderstorm cloud — [WMO Cloud Atlas: cloud classification summary](https://cloudatlas.wmo.int/en/cloud-classification-summary.html) (search extract)
- Nimbostratus: "Grey cloud layer, often dark, the appearance of which is rendered diffuse by more or less continuously falling rain or snow, which, in most cases, reaches the ground. It is thick enough throughout to blot out the Sun. Low, ragged clouds frequently occur below the layer, with which they may or may not merge." — [WMO Cloud Atlas: Nimbostratus](https://cloudatlas.wmo.int/en/nimbostratus-ns.html) (search extract, quoted)
- The cloud "is identified as Nimbostratus when precipitation (rain, snow or ice pellets) reaches the ground" (distinguishing it from altostratus) — [WMO Cloud Atlas: Ns compared with As](https://cloudatlas.wmo.int/en/ns-compared-with-as-op.html) (search extract)
- Oktas: 0 means clear sky, 8 means completely overcast, and 9 means the sky is obscured, for example by fog. Met Office terms: 3–4 oktas is "scattered", 5–7 is "broken", and 8 is "overcast". The Met Office classifies cloud as low, medium or high following WMO criteria — [Met Office: How we measure cloud](https://weather.metoffice.gov.uk/guides/observations/how-we-measure-cloud); [Wikipedia: Okta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Okta) (search extracts)
- Met Office *model* level bands (approximate, not observing rules): low about 111–1,949 m, medium about 1,949–5,574 m, high about 5,574–13,608 m — [Photo Ephemeris forum quoting Met Office model documentation](https://forum.photoephemeris.com/t/using-the-multi-level-cloud-forecast-layer/917) (low-quality secondary source)

### Inferences
- For the drone flight, the textbook "étage" ranges (temperate latitudes) are: low cloud (St, Sc, Cu, Cb bases) from the surface to about 2 km; middle (Ac, As, Ns usually) about 2–7 km; high (Ci, Cc, Cs) about 5–13 km. In the tropics, including Zimbabwe, the middle and high étages extend higher, up to about 18 km. **These ranges come from training knowledge of the Cloud Atlas and were not verified in this session; check them at cloudatlas.wmo.int before use.**
- Nimbostratus is usually placed in the middle étage but commonly extends into the low and high étages. Cumulonimbus has a low base and can reach the tropopause, which is about 16–18 km in the tropics. Treat these as unverified.
- Precipitation by cloud type for the animation: Ns gives continuous moderate rain. Cb gives heavy showers, thunder and hail. St gives drizzle at most. Fair-weather Cu usually gives none. Ci gives none at the ground.

### Gaps
- Full Cloud Atlas text for St, Cu, Cb and Ci (heights, species, precipitation) could not be fetched.
- In Zimbabwe, nimbostratus-type continuous rain is less typical than convective Cb storms. Continuous rain occurs in tropical-temperate trough or cloud-band events and on the Eastern Highlands, where it is called "guti" drizzle. The "guti" detail is training knowledge and was not verified.

## 3. Processes: dew point, condensation, adiabatic cooling, precipitation formation, rainfall types

### Takeaway
Rising air expands and cools at about 9.8 °C/km until it reaches its dew point. Above that, condensation on nuclei releases latent heat, so cooling slows to about 4–9 °C/km depending on temperature, typically 5–6 °C/km in mid-levels. Droplets grow into rain by collision–coalescence in warm clouds. In clouds that rise above the freezing level, rain forms through the Bergeron ice-crystal process. Uplift comes from convection, relief or fronts.

### Cited Findings
- Dry adiabatic lapse rate: 9.8 °C/km (5.4 °F per 1,000 ft). The saturated rate is lower because condensation releases latent heat of vaporisation. It is about 4 °C/km at 20 °C and close to 9 °C/km at −40 °C — [Wikipedia: Lapse rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lapse_rate) (search extract)
- Stability: air is stable if the environmental lapse rate is less than the adiabatic rate and unstable if it is greater. For saturated air, compare against the moist rate — [Wikipedia: Lapse rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lapse_rate) (search extract)
- Dew point is the temperature at which air becomes saturated (RH = 100%) — [Britannica: psychrometer](https://britannica.com/technology/psychrometer) and related search extracts
- Bergeron–Findeisen process: in mixed clouds with many supercooled drops and a few ice crystals, the crystals grow by vapour deposition at the drops' expense. This happens because saturation vapour pressure over ice is lower than over supercooled water. The drops evaporate and the vapour deposits on the ice — [AMS Glossary: Bergeron–Findeisen process](https://glossary.ametsoc.org/wiki/bergeron-findeisen-process/); [College of DuPage: Collision/Coalescence; The Bergeron Process](https://weather.cod.edu/sirvatka/bergeron.html); [Penn State METEO 300](https://www.e-education.psu.edu/meteo300/node/781)
- Supercooled water and ice coexist roughly between −10 and −40 °C. Collision–coalescence, in which drops collide and merge, is the mechanism in warm (ice-free) clouds — [Plymouth State precipitation tutorial](https://vortex.plymouth.edu/dept/tutorials/precip/precip2aa.html); [College of DuPage](https://weather.cod.edu/sirvatka/bergeron.html)
- Convectional rain: local showers and thunderstorms caused by localised heating of the ground. Relief rain: on the windward side air cools near the summit, forming thick cloud and rain. Rain shadow: on the lee side air descends and warms, giving little or no rain. Frontal rain: warm air rises over cold air, and at a warm front it gives lighter, prolonged rain — [MetLink (Royal Meteorological Society): Rainfall and relief](https://www.metlink.org/resource/in-depth-rainfall-and-relief); [Met Office "Why does it rain" transcript](https://acct.metoffice.gov.uk/binaries/content/assets/metofficegovuk/migrated/pdf/4/transcript_why_does_it_rain.pdf); [The Geo Room (Zimbabwe syllabus site): precipitation](https://www.thegeoroom.co.zw/climatology/precipitation.php) (search extracts)

### Inferences
- **Worked example for the drone animation (illustrative numbers, not observed data):** surface 30 °C and dew point 18 °C on a Zimbabwe summer afternoon. The cloud base forms where the parcel cools to its dew point, roughly at 125 m per °C of spread (rule-of-thumb LCL ≈ 125 × (T − Td)). That gives a base of about 1.5 km above ground. Above the base, cooling continues at about 5–6 °C/km. The 125 m/°C rule was not sourced in this session, so verify it before use.
- Zimbabwe's plateau sits about 1,000–1,500 m above sea level, so cloud bases above sea level are correspondingly higher. This is unverified.

### Gaps
- No primary NOAA/NWS JetStream page was retrieved for these figures. The CCN size and number figures (CCN about 0.2 µm, cloud droplet about 20 µm, raindrop about 2 mm; about 1 million droplets per raindrop) are commonly cited but were not verified here.

## 4. Zimbabwe climate context

### Takeaway
The main rains fall from about November to March. Most of the rain (about 90%) is convective thunderstorm rain linked to the ITCZ and summer heating; the rest is orographic and frontal. The Eastern Highlands are the wettest area (over 1,000 mm, locally over 1,200 mm). Five Natural Regions, defined mainly by rainfall, run from Region I (over 1,000 mm) to Region V (under about 450–500 mm). Masvingo is semi-arid (about 625 mm) and lies mostly in Natural Region IV, with Region V in the south.

### Cited Findings
- Rainy season: about November to March, with a post-rainy period March–May. The whole country is influenced by the ITCZ during January. When the ITCZ is poorly defined, rainfall is below average and serious drought is likely — [Geography of Zimbabwe (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geography_of_Zimbabwe); [GlobalSecurity: Zimbabwe climate](https://www.globalsecurity.org/military/world/africa/zw-climate.htm) (search extracts)
- 90% of the country's precipitation is driven by convective forces, with the rest linked to orography and frontal systems. In October–December, westerly cloud bands bring rain — [Finnish Red Cross climate profile: Zimbabwe](https://www.punainenristi.fi/globalassets/6.-tyomme--paasivu/kansainvalinen-apu/kehitysyhteistyo/ilmastoriskit/climate-profiles-of-countries-in-southern-africa-zimbabwe.pdf) (search extract)
- Rain-bearing systems besides the ITCZ include westerly cloud bands and Indian Ocean tropical cyclones. A 1980–2013 analysis suggests the summer season may be shortening — [South African Journal of Science: Characterisation of wet and dry summer seasons over Zimbabwe](https://sajs.co.za/article/view/6517) (search extract)
- The Eastern Highlands are the wettest region, with over 1,000 mm and only parts receiving over 1,200 mm a year — [Geography of Zimbabwe (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geography_of_Zimbabwe) (search extract)
- Natural Regions are based mainly on rainfall, plus soils and vegetation, and land quality declines from I to V — [FAO a0395e](https://www.fao.org/3/a0395e/a0395e06.htm). Rainfall bands differ between FAO documents:
  - **I:** over 1,000 mm, rain in all months; about 1.56% of land — [FAO a0395e](https://www.fao.org/3/a0395e/a0395e06.htm); [FAO x5598e](https://www.fao.org/4/x5598e/x5598e07.htm)
  - **II:** 700–1,000 mm ([FAO x5598e](https://www.fao.org/4/x5598e/x5598e07.htm)) or 700–1,050 mm ([FAO x5594e](https://www.fao.org/3/X5594E/X5594e03.htm)); summer rain only. IIa is 750–1,000 mm according to the [USDA drought-prone areas document](https://ipad.fas.usda.gov/highlights/2009/06/zimbabwe/documents/drought_prone_areas.pdf)
  - **III:** 650–800 mm ([FAO x5598e](https://www.fao.org/4/x5598e/x5598e07.htm); [USDA](https://ipad.fas.usda.gov/highlights/2009/06/zimbabwe/documents/drought_prone_areas.pdf)) or 500–700 mm ([FAO x5594e](https://www.fao.org/3/X5594E/X5594e03.htm)); erratic rain with mid-season drought
  - **IV:** 450–600 mm, frequent seasonal drought, about a third of the country — [FAO x5594e](https://www.fao.org/3/X5594E/X5594e03.htm)
  - **V:** under 450 mm ([FAO x5598e](https://www.fao.org/4/x5598e/x5598e07.htm)) or normally under 500 mm ([FAO x5594e](https://www.fao.org/3/X5594E/X5594e03.htm)); very erratic
- Regions III–V hold about 80% of the rural population — [FAO x5594e](https://www.fao.org/3/X5594E/X5594e03.htm). Boundaries have shifted with climate variability: Region II shrank by 49% and Region III by 13.9%, while Regions I, IV and V grew — [Mugandani et al. 2012, African Crop Science Journal](https://www.ajol.info/index.php/acsj/article/view/81761)
- Masvingo: average annual rainfall about 625 mm — [Weather Spark (Masvingo)](https://de.weatherspark.com/countries/ZW/08) (search extract; climate aggregator). The province is lowveld or tropical savannah where rainfall is "minimal and uncertain", and much of the south is drought-prone Region V — [Wikipedia: Masvingo Province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masvingo_Province) (search extract)
- Masvingo District (1988/89 data): NR III 7%, NR IV 82%, NR V 11% of area — [FAO AB967E](https://www.fao.org/4/AB967E/AB967E10.htm)
- Devure is a ward of Bikita District, Masvingo Province, at about 20° S, 32.33° E — [Wikipedia: Devure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devure) (search extract)
- Meteorological Services Department (MSD) of Zimbabwe: it issues seasonal forecasts following the SARCOF regional outlook. For 2024–25 it moved to rolling, overlapping three-month periods (OND, NDJ, DJF, JFM) and runs a cloud-seeding programme when required. Director Rebecca Manzou announced a normal to above-normal 2025–26 forecast. The MSD expected Masvingo, Matabeleland and the Midlands to be most affected by low rains in 2023–24 — [AllAfrica (Nov 2025)](https://allafrica.com/stories/202511050145.html); [NewsDay: 2024/25 rainfall forecast](https://newsday.co.zw/opinion-analysis/article/200032126/202425-rainfall-forecast-signals-challenges-opportunities); [NewsDay: Drought looms in southern Zim](https://newsday.co.zw/local/article/200016886/drought-looms-in-southern-zim) (news reports, not MSD documents)

### Inferences
- Bikita lies east of Masvingo town, toward the Save valley. It plausibly spans wetter higher ground on its western and northern side (around NR III/IV) and drier Save lowlands (NR IV/V). It also sits on the leeward or dry side relative to the Eastern Highlands for the moist easterlies, which makes it a reasonable rain-shadow example. This is NOT verified by any source found.
- For the film, a typical Masvingo/Bikita November–March afternoon is the best scenario: hot morning, thermals, towering cumulus becoming cumulonimbus, then a short heavy thunderstorm. This matches the "90% convective" finding better than a nimbostratus scene. A nimbostratus or continuous-rain scene fits a cloud-band day or the Eastern Highlands.

### Gaps
- No source gave Bikita District's own Natural Region classification or mean annual rainfall. MSD station data, for example Masvingo or Zaka/Bikita rain stations, would be needed.
- The msd.gov.zw site itself was not retrieved. The MSD's official name, founding, and the number of stations or school-station programmes are unverified.
- There is no detailed source on the Eastern Highlands' orographic mechanism (SE trade winds and the Chimanimani/Nyanga escarpment) or on "guti".
- The season start date conflicts between sources (October vs November).
