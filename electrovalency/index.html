ELECTROVALENCY

Electron transfer

⚡ Electron transfer: how an ionic bond begins!

In an electrovalent (ionic) bond, sodium gives its 1 outer electron to chlorine. Both end with a full outer shell, as ions Na⁺ and Cl⁻.

1️⃣ Sodium atom, Na: 2,8,1 · a metal · gives away 1 electron
2️⃣ 1 electron moves: transferred from Na to Cl
3️⃣ Chlorine atom, Cl: 2,8,7 · a non-metal · takes 1 electron

🧪 Na 2,8,1 → Na⁺ 2,8 + e⁻ · Cl 2,8,7 + e⁻ → Cl⁻ 2,8,8
💡 Electrons always move from the metal to the non-metal.

ELECTROVALENCY

Give & take

🤝 Give & take: what electrovalency really means!

Electrovalency (ionic bonding) is the transfer of electrons from a metal to a non-metal. It makes oppositely charged ions that attract each other.

1️⃣ Positive ion, Na⁺: the metal lost 1 electron
2️⃣ Negative ion, Cl⁻: the non-metal gained 1 electron
3️⃣ Ionic bond: + and − ions attract each other

🧪 metal loses e⁻ → cation (+) · non-metal gains e⁻ → anion (−)
💡 Electrovalent and ionic bonding mean the same thing.

REDOX

OIL RIG

🔄 OIL RIG: Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain!

Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain of electrons. When sodium meets chlorine, sodium is oxidised and chlorine is reduced.

1️⃣ Na loses 1 e⁻: loss of electrons = oxidation
2️⃣ Cl gains 1 e⁻: gain of electrons = reduction
3️⃣ Redox: both happen at the same time

🧪 Na → Na⁺ + e⁻ (oxidation) · Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻ (reduction)
💡 Every electrovalent bond forms through a redox reaction.

METALS

Metals give

🔩 Metals give: why metals lose electrons!

Metals have only 1, 2 or 3 outer electrons. Losing them is far easier than gaining 5 or more, so metals always form positive ions.

1️⃣ Outer electron leaves: only 1, far from the nucleus
2️⃣ Na atom, 2,8,1: a Group I metal
3️⃣ Becomes Na⁺, 2,8: the shell underneath is full

🧪 Na → Na⁺ + e⁻ · Mg → Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻ · Al → Al³⁺ + 3e⁻
💡 Group I → 1+, Group II → 2+, Group III → 3+.

NON-METALS

Non-metals take

🧲 Non-metals take: why non-metals gain electrons!

Non-metals have 5, 6 or 7 outer electrons. Gaining 1, 2 or 3 more fills the shell, so they form negative ions.

1️⃣ 1 electron arrives: from the metal atom
2️⃣ Cl atom, 2,8,7: 7 outer electrons: room for 1
3️⃣ Becomes Cl⁻, 2,8,8: now a full outer shell

🧪 Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻ · O + 2e⁻ → O²⁻ · N + 3e⁻ → N³⁻
💡 Group VII → 1−, Group VI → 2−, Group V → 3−.

IONS

Cation & anion

➕ Cation & anion: never mix them up again!

Ions are atoms that have lost or gained electrons. Losing electrons makes a positive cation; gaining them makes a negative anion.

1️⃣ Cation (+): lost electrons · usually a metal
2️⃣ Anion (−): gained electrons · a non-metal
3️⃣ -ide ending: chlorine → chloride ion

🧪 Na → Na⁺ (cation) · Cl → Cl⁻ (anion: chloride)
💡 Memory tip: the t in caTion looks like a plus sign (+).

ION SIZES

Ion sizes

📏 Ion sizes: why Na⁺ shrinks and Cl⁻ grows!

Losing its outer shell makes Na⁺ much smaller than a sodium atom. Extra electrons repel each other, so Cl⁻ is larger than a chlorine atom.

1️⃣ Na⁺ ≈ 204 pm: smaller than a Na atom (372 pm)
2️⃣ Cl⁻ ≈ 362 pm: larger than a Cl atom (198 pm)
3️⃣ Rule to remember: cations shrink, anions grow

🧪 Na 372 pm → Na⁺ 204 pm · Cl 198 pm → Cl⁻ 362 pm
💡 Sizes are diameters, measured across the particle.

IONIC BOND

Opposites attract

🧲 Opposites attract: the force that holds salt together!

The ionic bond is the strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions. It pulls equally in every direction.

1️⃣ Na⁺, positive: 11 protons, 10 electrons
2️⃣ Cl⁻, negative: 17 protons, 18 electrons
3️⃣ Electrostatic attraction: pulls the ions together: the bond

🧪 Na⁺ + Cl⁻ → NaCl · like charges repel, opposite charges attract
💡 Protons stay the same: only electrons move.

OCTET

Full shells

🎱 Full shells: why ions are so stable!

After the transfer both ions have the same electron arrangement as a noble gas. Full outer shells are very stable.

1️⃣ Na⁺: 2,8: the same as neon, Ne
2️⃣ Cl⁻: 2,8,8: the same as argon, Ar
3️⃣ Stable at last: full shells, like the noble gases

🧪 Na⁺ 2,8 = Ne · Cl⁻ 2,8,8 = Ar · Mg²⁺ 2,8 = Ne · O²⁻ 2,8 = Ne
💡 With full shells, Na⁺ and Cl⁻ have no reason to react further.

CaCl₂

Calcium chloride

🧪 Calcium chloride: one metal, two non-metals!

Calcium (2,8,8,2) has 2 electrons to give, but each chlorine needs only 1. So one calcium bonds with two chlorines.

1️⃣ Chlorine, Cl: takes 1 electron → Cl⁻
2️⃣ Calcium, Ca: 2,8,8,2 · gives 2 electrons → Ca²⁺
3️⃣ Chlorine, Cl: takes 1 electron → Cl⁻

🧪 Ca → Ca²⁺ + 2e⁻ · 2Cl + 2e⁻ → 2Cl⁻ · Ca²⁺ + 2Cl⁻ → CaCl₂
💡 Calcium is in Group II, so it always forms Ca²⁺.

MgCl₂

Magnesium chloride

🧪 Magnesium chloride: the Group II pattern!

Group II metals give away 2 electrons. Magnesium needs two chlorine atoms to take them, one each, making MgCl₂.

1️⃣ One each: 1 electron to each chlorine atom
2️⃣ Magnesium, Mg: 2,8,2 · gives away 2 electrons
3️⃣ Cl → Cl⁻: each chlorine gains 1 electron

🧪 Mg → Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻ · Mg²⁺ + 2Cl⁻ → MgCl₂
💡 Same pattern: CaCl₂, BaCl₂ and MgBr₂ each have one 2+ ion and two 1− ions.

MgO

Magnesium oxide

💪 Magnesium oxide: two electrons, one strong bond!

Here 2 electrons move from one magnesium atom to one oxygen atom. The doubly charged ions attract very strongly.

1️⃣ Mg²⁺ (2,8): lost 2 electrons
2️⃣ O²⁻ (2,8): gained 2 electrons

FORMULAS

Swap & drop

✍️ Swap & drop: write any ionic formula in seconds!

Write each ion with its charge, swap the numbers and drop the signs. Then check that the charges cancel.

1️⃣ Ca²⁺: charge 2+ (Group II)
2️⃣ Cl⁻: charge 1− (Group VII)
3️⃣ Swap & drop: Ca₁Cl₂ → write CaCl₂

🧪 Ca²⁺ Cl⁻ → CaCl₂ · Al³⁺ O²⁻ → Al₂O₃ · Na⁺ O²⁻ → Na₂O
💡 Leave out the number 1: CaCl₂, not Ca₁Cl₂.

CHARGE

Charges balance

⚖️ Charges balance: why the formula is CaCl₂!

An ionic compound has no overall charge. In calcium chloride, one Ca²⁺ (+2) and two Cl⁻ (−1 each) add up to zero.

1️⃣ Cl⁻: −1: one chloride ion
2️⃣ Ca²⁺: +2: one calcium ion
3️⃣ Cl⁻: −1: a second chloride ion

🧪 (+2) + (−1) + (−1) = 0 · so the formula is CaCl₂
💡 Always check: total + equals total −.

CATIONS

Positive ions

➕ Positive ions: the cations you must know!

Metal atoms lose their outer electrons to become cations. The size of the charge equals the number of electrons lost.

1️⃣ 1+ ions: Li⁺ Na⁺ K⁺ · also Ag⁺, NH₄⁺
2️⃣ Cation = + ion: a metal atom that lost electrons
3️⃣ 2+ and 3+ ions: Mg²⁺ Ca²⁺ Zn²⁺ Cu²⁺ · Al³⁺ Fe³⁺

🧪 Group I → 1+ · Group II → 2+ · Group III → 3+
💡 NH₄⁺ (ammonium) is the odd one out: a positive ion made only of non-metals.

ANIONS

Negative ions

➖ Negative ions: the anions you must know!

Non-metal atoms gain electrons to fill their outer shell, forming anions. Their names change to end in -ide: chloride, oxide, sulfide.

1️⃣ Anion = − ion: a non-metal atom that gained electrons
2️⃣ 1− ions: F⁻ Cl⁻ Br⁻ I⁻ · also OH⁻, NO₃⁻
3️⃣ 2− and 3− ions: O²⁻ S²⁻ · SO₄²⁻ CO₃²⁻ · N³⁻

🧪 Group VII → 1− · Group VI → 2− · Group V → 3−
💡 Polyatomic anions such as SO₄²⁻ keep their own names: sulfate.

VALENCY

Valency numbers

🔢 Valency numbers: the number behind every formula!

An element's electrovalency is the number of electrons its atom loses or gains. It equals the size of the charge on its ion.

1️⃣ Ca: valency 2: loses 2 electrons → Ca²⁺
2️⃣ 1 arrow = 1 e⁻: each chlorine takes one
3️⃣ Cl: valency 1: gains 1 electron → Cl⁻

🧪 Na 1 · Mg 2 · Al 3 · Cl 1 · O 2 · N 3
💡 Na, K, Cl: valency 1 · Mg, Ca, O: valency 2 · Al, N: valency 3.

LATTICE

Giant lattice

🧊 Giant lattice: what solid salt looks like inside!

Solid sodium chloride is a giant ionic lattice: millions of Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions packed in a regular cubic pattern.

1️⃣ Na⁺ ions: small yellow spheres (+)
2️⃣ Cl⁻ ions: large blue spheres (−)
3️⃣ Giant lattice: repeats in all 3 directions

🧪 Na⁺ : Cl⁻ = 1 : 1 · formula NaCl
💡 There are no NaCl molecules, just one huge lattice of ions.

LATTICE

Six neighbours

🔬 Six neighbours: how the ions pack together!

In the sodium chloride lattice every ion is surrounded by six ions of the opposite charge, at right angles to each other.

1️⃣ Each Cl⁻ ion: is touched by 6 Na⁺ ions
2️⃣ Each Na⁺ ion: is touched by 6 Cl⁻ ions
3️⃣ 6 : 6 arrangement: up, down, left, right, front, back

🧪 Na⁺: 6 Cl⁻ neighbours · Cl⁻: 6 Na⁺ neighbours
💡 Chemists call this 6:6 coordination.

PROPERTIES

Hard to melt

🔥 Hard to melt: why salt never melts in your kitchen!

Ionic compounds have high melting and boiling points because a huge number of strong attractions must be overcome.

1️⃣ Strong attractions: every ion pulls on its neighbours
2️⃣ Lots of energy: needed to break the lattice apart
3️⃣ NaCl melts at 801 °C: MgO at 2852 °C

🧪 NaCl 801 °C · KCl 770 °C · MgO 2852 °C · ice 0 °C
💡 Ice (made of covalent water molecules) melts at just 0 °C.

ELECTROLYSIS

Conducts when free

💡 Conducts when free: when salt carries electricity!

Ions carry electric charge, but only when they can move: in molten salt or salt solution, never in the solid.

1️⃣ Solid: no current: ions locked in the lattice
2️⃣ Na⁺ moves to (−): when molten or dissolved
3️⃣ Cl⁻ moves to (+): when molten or dissolved

🧪 solid NaCl: no · molten NaCl: yes · NaCl solution: yes
💡 Cations go to the cathode (−), anions to the anode (+).

PROPERTIES

Brittle crystals

💥 Brittle crystals: why salt crystals shatter!

Hit an ionic crystal and one layer slides. Ions with the same charge line up side by side, repel each other, and the crystal shatters.

1️⃣ A sharp blow: pushes one layer of ions along
2️⃣ Like charges meet: + lines up with +, − with −
3️⃣ Repulsion: the layers push apart: it shatters

🧪 blow → layer slides → like charges meet → repulsion → crack
💡 Metals bend instead: their layers slide without repelling.

DIAGRAMS

Dot & cross

✏️ Dot & cross: draw ionic bonds the exam way!

Exam style: draw each ion in square brackets with its charge outside. Use dots for one atom's electrons and crosses for the other's.

1️⃣ Outer shell only: Cl⁻: 7 × of its own + 1 • from Na
2️⃣ Brackets + charge: write [Cl]⁻, charge at top right
3️⃣ [Na]⁺: outer shell now empty: draw it bare

🧪 [Na]⁺ [Cl]⁻ · Cl⁻ outer shell: 7 × + 1 • = 8
💡 Show only the outer shell unless the question asks for all shells.

EVERYDAY

Table salt

🧂 Table salt: the ionic compound on your table!

Every grain of table salt is a tiny crystal built from Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions. The cubic lattice is why salt grains look like little boxes.

1️⃣ Na⁺ and Cl⁻: in a 1 : 1 ratio
2️⃣ Cube-shaped: salt grains are tiny cubes
3️⃣ ≈ 10¹⁸ ions: in one grain of table salt

🧪 sodium chloride · NaCl · melts at 801 °C · dissolves in water
💡 Seawater is about 3.5% dissolved salts, mostly sodium chloride.

COVALENT

Sharing, not giving

🫧 Sharing, not giving: when atoms share instead!

Two non-metal atoms both want to gain electrons, so neither gives any away. They share instead: a covalent bond, not an electrovalent one.

1️⃣ Oxygen atom, O: 2,6 · needs 2 more electrons
2️⃣ Oxygen atom, O: also needs 2: neither will give
3️⃣ 2 shared pairs: a double covalent bond, O=O

🧪 O + O → O=O (O₂) · 2 shared pairs = double bond · no ions formed
💡 Covalent bonds make small molecules like O₂, H₂O and CO₂.

COMPARE

Ionic vs covalent

🆚 Ionic vs covalent: spot the difference!

Quick test: is there a metal? Metal + non-metal usually means ionic. Only non-metals usually means covalent.

1️⃣ Ionic (electrovalent): electrons transferred · metal + non-metal · giant lattice · high melting point · conducts when molten or dissolved
2️⃣ Covalent: electrons shared · non-metals only · small molecules · low melting point · does not conduct

🧪 ionic: NaCl, MgO, CaCl₂ · covalent: H₂O, CO₂, O₂
